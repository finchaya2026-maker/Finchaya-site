Death Cover or Survival Benefit? Which Life Insurance Should You Buy

19 October 2026 · 9 min read

Every life insurance policy sold in India answers one of two questions. What does my family get if I die? Or: what do I get if I live? A term plan answers only the first. An endowment, money-back or guaranteed-return plan tries to answer both, and that is where most of the confusion, and most of the disappointment, comes from.

Most Indian households own the second kind and not the first. A Swiss Re study found that for every ₹100 an Indian family would need if the earner died, it had about ₹8 in savings and insurance combined. That study is from 2015, but ask around: how many people you know hold a ₹1 crore term plan, and how many hold a policy "for ₹10 lakh" bought at 25?

The short answer. If anyone depends on your income, buy death cover first: a plain term plan for 10 to 15 times your yearly income, plus your loans. It is the only product that pays your family a sum they can actually live on. A survival-benefit plan is a savings product with a small life cover attached. It can make sense for a specific kind of saver, after the term plan is in place and the usual tax-free options are full. It is never a substitute for the term plan.

The two kinds, side by side

Death cover is sold as a term plan: you pay a small premium every year for a fixed term, say until you are 60. If you die in that time, your family gets the sum assured. If you live, you get nothing back. That "nothing" is exactly why it is cheap.

Survival benefit comes in several wrappers: endowment plans (a lump sum at the end), money-back plans (instalments along the way), guaranteed-return plans (a promised sum or income), and ULIPs (market-linked, with charges). All of them bundle a life cover with a savings plan, and the premium mostly goes to the savings part.

The arithmetic with ₹1 lakh a year

Take a 30-year-old with ₹1 lakh a year to spend on "insurance". Here are the two routes over 20 years.

  • Route 1, survival benefit: an endowment plan at ₹1 lakh a year, sum assured about ₹20 lakh, maturity after 20 years.
  • Route 2, death cover plus savings: a ₹1 crore term plan at about ₹14,000 a year, and the remaining ₹86,000 a year invested separately.

Read the first row twice. The family that bought "savings with insurance" gets about ₹23 lakh when it needs money most. The family that bought insurance gets five times that. The second row shows that the endowment does not even win on savings: a plain PPF, which is also tax-free and government-backed, ends up ahead, and an index fund ends up far ahead.

This is the whole case for "buy term, invest the rest". It is not a clever trick; it is what happens when you stop paying an insurer to do your saving.

When death cover is the right choice

Almost always, and first. Buy a term plan if:

  • Someone depends on your income: a spouse, children, parents. This is the only test that matters.
  • You have a home loan or any large loan. A term plan for at least the loan amount means the bank does not take the house from your family.
  • You are young and healthy. Premiums are set by your age and health at purchase and then frozen for the term. A 30-year-old pays roughly half of what a 40-year-old pays for the same cover. Buying late is the most expensive mistake in insurance.
  • You are the only earner. Then cover 15 times your income, not 10.

How much? A workable rule: 10 to 15 times your yearly income, plus outstanding loans, minus investments your family could actually use. Run the term until your youngest child is likely to be financially independent, or until you retire, whichever is later. Do not buy a "whole life" term to 99; after 65 nobody depends on your salary, and the premium reflects the certainty of a claim.

When a survival-benefit plan can make sense

Rarely, and second. The honest cases:

  • You will not save unless the money is locked away. Some people genuinely need the discipline of a premium notice and a surrender penalty. A 5% return that actually gets saved beats a 10% return that gets spent. If that is you, know that you are paying for discipline, not for insurance.
  • You want a guaranteed, tax-free return for 15–20 years and the usual tax-free options are full. PPF takes ₹1.5 lakh a year, Sukanya Samriddhi another ₹1.5 lakh for a daughter. Beyond that, a guaranteed-return plan paying 5.5%–6.5% tax-free can compete with a bank FD for someone in the 30% bracket (a 6% tax-free return equals an 8.7% FD before tax). Two conditions: your term plan is already in place, and your total life-insurance premiums stay at or under ₹5 lakh a year, or the maturity becomes taxable and the case collapses.
  • You want a sum that reaches a nominee outside the estate, or protected from creditors. A policy under the Married Women's Property Act does this. So does a term plan, more cheaply.
  • You are certain you can pay every premium for the whole term. A savings plan surrendered in year 6 is one of the worst investments in India. If your income is irregular, stay away.

Notice what is not on this list: "I want insurance and returns together." You can have both. Just not from the same product, and not at a good price.

Four traps

  1. Treating the endowment as your insurance. It is a savings plan. If it is the only policy you own, your family is covered for a year or two of expenses, not a lifetime.
  2. "Return of premium" term plans. They refund your premiums if you survive, and charge roughly double for it. The refund is your own money returned after 30 years at zero interest. Put the extra ₹14,000 a year in PPF instead and you would have about ₹14 lakh at the end, versus an ₹8.4 lakh refund.
  3. Buying the cover you can afford, not the cover you need. ₹1 crore of cover through an endowment would cost ₹5–7 lakh a year, so people buy ₹10 lakh and feel insured. Through a term plan the same ₹1 crore costs a fortnight's salary.
  4. Surrendering a savings plan to buy a term plan. Buy the term plan first, from a separate budget. Then decide, with the surrender value in hand, whether the old policy is worth continuing. Often a policy past its halfway mark is worth keeping to maturity; one in its first few years usually is not.

The decision in three questions

  1. Does anyone depend on your income? Yes: buy a term plan for 10–15 times your income plus loans, this month. No: you may not need life insurance at all.
  2. Is that term plan in place? If not, stop here. Nothing else in life insurance matters until it is.
  3. Do you have spare money you want locked for 15–20 years at a guaranteed 5%–6% tax-free, after PPF, EPF and your equity SIPs are funded? Then a guaranteed-return or endowment plan can be a small slice, with total premiums under ₹5 lakh a year. If the answer is "I'm not sure", the answer is no.

The bottom line

Death cover and survival benefit are not two flavours of the same thing. One is insurance; the other is a deposit wearing an insurance badge. Buy the insurance for what insurance is for: a sum large enough that your family's life does not change if yours ends. Then save, separately, in products built for saving. The households that get this backwards end up with policies that pay too little when they die and return too little when they live.

Should Your Emergency Fund Sit in an Arbitrage Fund?

18 October 2026 · 9 min read

A hospital deposit. A sudden job loss. A car that dies the week the rent is due. Emergencies don't send a notice, and yet survey after survey finds that only about one Indian in four has a proper emergency fund set aside.

Those who do usually keep it in one of two places: a bank fixed deposit (FD) or a liquid fund. There is a third option that many planners quietly use: the arbitrage fund. It looks like a liquid fund and, on most days, behaves like one. But it is taxed very differently. Is it a smarter home for your safety money? We checked it against up to 10 years of real NAV data.

The short answer. For the bulk of an emergency fund, an arbitrage fund can beat both an FD and a liquid fund after tax, if you are in the 20% or 30% tax bracket and will not need the money within a year. But it should not hold all of it. Keep the first one to two months of expenses in a savings account or liquid fund, where money arrives the same day or next day, and let an arbitrage fund carry the rest.

What an emergency fund must do

An emergency fund is not an investment. It is insurance you pay for in lost returns. So it has three jobs, in this order:

  1. Be there when you need it. Money in your hand within a day or two, with no penalty.
  2. Not shrink. If you need ₹3 lakh, the fund must not be ₹2.8 lakh that week.
  3. Earn a little, after tax. A distant third. This is the only job where arbitrage funds claim an edge.

The usual rule of thumb is six months of essential expenses (rent, EMIs, groceries, insurance premiums, school fees), and nine to twelve months if your income is irregular. Keep that in mind as we go: the question is never "which option earns the most?" but "which one earns the most without failing jobs 1 and 2?"

An arbitrage fund in plain English

An arbitrage fund locks in a small, near-certain price gap between two markets. Here is the idea with illustrative numbers:

  • A share trades at ₹1,000 in the cash market. The same share's monthly futures contract trades at ₹1,006.
  • The fund buys the share at ₹1,000 and, at the same moment, sells the futures at ₹1,006.
  • On expiry day the two prices must meet. Whether the share ends at ₹900 or ₹1,100, the fund keeps the ₹6 gap (about 0.6%), before costs.

Because the buy and the sell cancel each other out, the stock market's ups and downs largely wash out. The fund repeats this across many stocks every month. The remaining money (up to about a third of the fund) sits in short-term debt and cash.

The key detail for your wallet: because at least 65% of the fund is in equity-related positions, the tax law treats it as an equity fund, even though it is built to behave like a debt fund. That one label is the whole story behind its appeal.

Advantage 1: you keep more of what you earn

This is the headline reason. Interest from an FD and gains from a liquid fund are added to your income and taxed at your slab rate. Gains from an arbitrage fund are taxed as equity gains:

  • 20% if you sell within 12 months.
  • 12.5% if you sell after 12 months, and the first ₹1.25 lakh of long-term equity gains in a financial year is tax-free.
  • You pay only when you sell. FD interest is taxed every year, whether you withdraw it or not.

Here is what ₹5 lakh would have left you with after one year, for someone in the 30% slab (31.2% with cess). We used the average pre-tax returns of our sample since January 2019: 5.85% for liquid funds and 6.39% for arbitrage funds.

A rough rule: if you will hold for over a year, arbitrage comes out ahead from the 15%–20% slab upward. If you might sell inside a year, it only clearly wins at the 30% slab, because the 20% tax on short-term gains is no better than a 20% slab.

Advantage 2: it rides like a liquid fund, and has earned a little more

You might expect a fund that touches the equity and futures markets to be bumpy. In practice, because every purchase is hedged, it has been remarkably calm. Over the history we have (up to 10 years):

  • Since January 2019, the arbitrage funds in our sample returned 6.2%–6.6% a year, against 5.8%–5.9% for the liquid funds. All before tax.
  • The worst 12-month return of any arbitrage fund was between 3.3% and 4.9%, about the same as the worst for liquid funds (3.3%).
  • Over any 30-day stretch, the biggest loss was 0.21%. Over any 3-month stretch, none of the five lost money.

That is the profile of a safe parking place: modest returns, no nasty surprises.

Advantage 3: no lock-in, no penalty, no yearly tax bill

Break an FD early and the bank typically cuts the interest rate by 0.5%–1% on the amount. With an arbitrage fund there is no lock-in at all. You can withdraw any part of the money on any working day. Most funds charge an exit load of 0.25% only if you redeem within the first 15 days (a few still use 30 days; always check the scheme document). After that it is nil.

There is a quieter benefit too. An FD pays you interest that is taxed each year, and the bank deducts 10% TDS once your interest with that bank crosses ₹50,000 (₹1 lakh for senior citizens). A mutual fund does neither. The money keeps compounding, and you settle the tax once, on the day you sell.

Disadvantage 1: it is not instant

This is the one that matters most for an emergency. A liquid fund pays out on the next working day, and some fund houses offer an instant-access facility of up to ₹50,000 (or 90% of your holding, whichever is lower) per day. An arbitrage fund generally takes 2–3 working days to credit your bank account, and it has no instant-access option.

In plain terms: if you redeem on a Friday evening, the money may reach you only on Tuesday or Wednesday. If the emergency is a hospital admission tonight, an arbitrage fund alone cannot help. This is exactly why the layered plan below keeps a first layer elsewhere.

One more trap: if you build your fund through a monthly SIP, each instalment has its own 15-day exit-load clock.

Disadvantage 2: the NAV wobbles

A bank balance never falls. A liquid fund's NAV almost never does (in our sample it ticked down on fewer than 1 day in 100). An arbitrage fund's NAV ticks down on roughly 3 days in 10, because the futures positions are marked to market every day.

These numbers are tiny. A 0.6% dip on ₹5 lakh is ₹3,000, and it was recovered. But "tiny" is not "zero", and certainty is the very thing an emergency fund is buying. If you happen to withdraw on a down day, you take that small loss.

Disadvantage 3: the edge is thin, and it depends on who you are

Three things can shrink or erase the advantage:

  • Your tax bracket. The whole benefit is the gap between your slab rate and the equity tax rate. At the 5% or 10% slab it can be negative. Under the new regime, income up to ₹12 lakh is effectively tax-free through the Section 87A rebate, so a liquid fund's gain may cost you nothing. That rebate does not apply to gains taxed at special equity rates, so an arbitrage fund sold within a year can be taxed at 20%. (Rules change, so confirm with your CA.)
  • The holding period. If you have to sell within 12 months, the 20% rate cuts deep into the benefit.
  • The pre-tax return itself. An arbitrage fund earns the gap between cash and futures prices. When that gap narrows, or when trading costs rise, returns fall. From 1 April 2026, Securities Transaction Tax on futures went from 0.02% to 0.05%. Analyst estimates of the drag on arbitrage fund returns range from about 0.2% to 0.4% a year. In our sample, 2026 so far (to early October) has been the first calendar year in a while where liquid funds edged ahead, at about 5.2% against 5.0%. One part-year proves nothing, but it shows that the pre-tax edge is not a promise.

So where should the money actually go?

Not in one place. Think of the emergency fund in two layers:

  • Layer 1: instant cash (about one to two months of expenses). A savings account, a sweep-in FD or a liquid fund. This covers the first 48 hours, when the hospital wants a deposit or the landlord wants the rent.
  • Layer 2: the rest. An arbitrage fund if you are in the 20%–30% bracket and the money is genuinely for emergencies. If you are in a lower bracket, a liquid fund or FD may serve you just as well.

For example, if your essential expenses are ₹50,000 a month, a six-month fund is ₹3 lakh: ₹1 lakh in Layer 1 and ₹2 lakh in Layer 2. This is an illustration, not a rule. The more you keep in Layer 1, the safer and quicker, but the less tax-efficient your fund becomes.

Who it suits, and who should skip it

An arbitrage fund is a good fit if:

  • You are in the 20% or 30% tax bracket.
  • Your emergency fund is large enough (a few lakh) for the tax saving to matter.
  • You already have Layer 1 cash in place, and a dip of half a percent would not rattle you.
  • You hold it for the long run and would only sell in a real emergency.

Think twice, or skip it, if:

  • Your taxable income is low, so a liquid fund or FD gain is taxed lightly or not at all.
  • The money is for a known expense within a year, such as a school fee or a house deposit. That is not an emergency fund.
  • Your fund is small (under about ₹1 lakh). The extra complexity isn't worth a saving of a few thousand rupees.
  • Certainty matters more to you than a percentage point. That is a perfectly good reason to choose an FD.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. Putting 100% in the arbitrage fund. You would have no money for the first two or three days of a crisis.
  2. Confusing it with look-alikes. Equity savings funds and balanced advantage funds sound similar, but they carry real, unhedged market risk. They are not emergency-fund material.
  3. Never testing it. Redeem a small amount once, say ₹1,000, and see how long it takes to reach your account. Also choose the Growth option: dividend (IDCW) payouts are taxed at your slab rate, which defeats the purpose.

The bottom line

An arbitrage fund is not a magic upgrade over an FD or a liquid fund. It is a trade: you accept slower access and a small, bumpy NAV, and in return you may keep about one more percentage point after tax, provided you are in a high tax bracket and hold for the long term. Used as the second layer of a two-layer emergency fund, that trade makes sense for many salaried and business-owner households. Used as the only layer, it doesn't.

Is your investment plan Market Smart? A Market Smart investor decides in advance which rupees are for safety and which are for growth, and never lets one do the other's job. Your emergency fund is the safety bucket. Keep it boring, keep it layered, and keep it separate from your SIPs.

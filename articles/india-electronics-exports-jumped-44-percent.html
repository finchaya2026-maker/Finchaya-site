India's Electronics Exports Jumped 44%. How Much of It Is Real?

21 November 2026 · 10 min read

On 17 November 2026 a widely shared post on X said that India's electronics exports had jumped by 44% to about US$32 billion in the first half of this financial year, and that exports of everything other than smartphones grew even faster. The official tables bear it out. So the real questions are what the number counts, where the jump came from, how much of it is simply dearer phones, and whether India is taking over from China.

The short answer. The 44% is real, but it is a US-dollar value, not a count of things made. About half of it is smartphones, mostly iPhones for the US. A good part of the phone growth is price: India-made phones sold into the US rose about 3% in number but about 25% in price per phone. Most of the "non-phone" growth sits in five product lines. And India is replacing China in one product in one market, smartphones in the US, not in electronics as a whole: China's share of world electronics exports has not fallen.

What the number counts

The figure is the Commerce Ministry's "Electronic Goods" group for April to September 2026, in US dollars, against April to September 2025. It counts goods leaving India, not services. It counts the full factory-gate value of a phone, not what India earns on it: industry estimates put the part of an Indian-assembled phone's value that is added in India at about 18% to 20%, and the rest is imported parts.

The post is accurate. Two things it leaves out: imports of electronic goods grew just as fast as exports, so the gap between them widened by about US$15 bn, and the dollar became about 11.6% dearer in rupees between April 2025 and October 2026 (Commerce's own conversion rate went from about Rs 85.6 to Rs 95.5), so in rupees the jump is even larger, 58.8%.

Where the jump came from

Of the US$9.77 bn increase, smartphones added US$4.79 bn and everything else US$4.98 bn. Phones are still 56.8% of the total, and iPhones are most of the phones: press reports citing Commerce data put Apple at about 75% to 82% of India's phone exports, which makes iPhones roughly 43% to 47% of all electronic goods exports (FinChaya calculation).

The non-phone half is more concentrated than it looks. Five product lines went from about US$1.05 bn to US$5.24 bn and explain 84% of that half's rise:

The biggest line, circuit-board parts sent to Singapore, grew about 33 times, and the ministry's statement does not say who ships it or why. The rest of non-phone electronics grew about 10%. The half-year also hides a gap between two quarters: non-phone exports grew about 21% in April to June and about 92% in July to September (FinChaya calculation). The comparison period matters too. July to September 2025 was weak, because the US put a 50% tariff on most Indian goods in August 2025, which flatters this year's growth rates.

How much of it is just dearer phones

A dollar figure rises when more things are sold or when each thing costs more. US customs data record both the number of phones and their value. Here is what happened to phones from India that entered the US, which takes about 70% of India's phone exports by value:

Almost all the growth in value is price and model mix, not more phones. The main reason is memory chips. Memory used to be about 12% to 13% of a smartphone's cost, and contract prices for the main type of memory chip rose by roughly 90% in the first quarter of 2026 and by more than half again in the second (TrendForce, as reported). Dixon Technologies, India's largest listed contract manufacturer, told analysts on 30 October that its volumes (phones and IT hardware) were slightly lower than a year earlier while its selling prices were up by roughly 35% to 40% for this reason. It also said India's own phone market fell 26% in the quarter, quoting industry reports.

None of this makes the jump fake. Apple really has moved assembly to India. It means a dollar figure can overstate how much more is being made, and it can fall back if memory prices fall.

Is India replacing China?

In smartphones sold to the US, yes:

The US did not start buying more phones. Its smartphone imports were US$59.1 bn in 2023, US$50.9 bn in 2024 and US$52.0 bn in 2025. The same demand was re-routed from China to India and Vietnam.

Across all electronics, the picture is larger and different. China's share of US electronics imports (including computers and parts) fell from 28.7% in 2023 to 6.3% in January to August 2026. But most of that went to others. In 2025, US electronics imports from Taiwan rose by about US$85 bn, from Mexico by US$47 bn, from Vietnam by US$44 bn, from Thailand by US$23 bn and from India by US$16 bn, as the US bought far more servers and computers. Inside the electronics chapter alone, US imports from Vietnam grew more than those from India, by US$19 bn against US$16 bn in 2025, and by US$21 bn against US$6 bn in January to August 2026 compared with the same months of 2025.

And China has not shrunk. Its share of world electronics exports was 24.9% in 2019 and 25.4% in 2025, and its exports of machinery and electrical goods, which include electronics, grew about 25% in January to October 2026 (China customs, reported by Xinhua). India's electronics exports on the same measure were US$53.6 bn in 2025, about 5% of China's US$1,016.6 bn.

One product, one market, one customer. India has taken a large share of the iPhones the US buys. That is real and it is big, but it is not the same as replacing China as the world's electronics factory.

Why it happened

  • Apple moved assembly. About 55 million iPhones were assembled in India in 2025, against about 36 million in 2024, roughly a quarter of Apple's global output (Bloomberg, as reported).
  • The government paid. The production-linked incentive (PLI) schemes for phones and IT hardware had paid out about Rs 15,554 crore by March 2026. It ended on 31 March 2026; a successor for phones, worth Rs 62,500 crore, was approved on 15 July 2026.
  • Tariffs favoured India in 2025. As reported, US tariffs that year weighed more on China-made phones than on India-made ones. That gap largely closed after the US Supreme Court struck down the emergency-power tariffs on 20 February 2026, and since 24 July 2026 smartphones are exempt from the new US duties. Yet India's share of US phone imports stayed between 51% and 67% in every month from March to August 2026, so the shift is not only a tariff trick.
  • A weaker rupee makes assembly cheaper in dollar terms, though it makes imported parts dearer.

What has to happen for it to last

  1. More of the phone has to be made in India. India imported US$81.34 bn of electronic goods in the same six months. The government's component scheme had approved 106 projects worth Rs 69,548 crore by August 2026, but only 38 plants were producing and 16 more were close (PIB, 17 September 2026). Approvals are plans, not output.
  2. Customers beyond Apple. With Apple at three-quarters or more of phone exports, one company's decisions can move the whole number.
  3. Incentives have to keep flowing. The old scheme has ended and the new one pays on sales above a baseline.
  4. Trade rules have to stay friendly. As of mid-November 2026 there is no signed India-US trade deal, and a US review of chip-related tariffs could reach electronics.
  5. Chip-making has to catch up. Five chip-related projects are in production in India, but none is a wafer plant; the first one is expected in 2028.

What this means if you invest

Rising exports do not mean every company that makes electronics in India earns more. Three reasons.

First, the biggest part of the Apple chain cannot be bought on the stock exchange. The largest iPhone makers in India, Tata Electronics and Foxconn's Indian units, are not listed.

Second, assembling a phone earns a thin margin. In company results for the September 2026 quarter, plain phone and IT assembly earned an operating margin of about 2% to 4%, while contract makers with design content earned about 9% to 13%. When prices rise and a company only passes the cost along, revenue grows and the margin shrinks.

Third, share prices and exports are different things. Over the year to 17 November 2026, among eight listed electronics contract manufacturers in the FinChaya database, three fell (by 16% to 54%) and five rose (by 37% to 90%), while exports rose throughout.

So when a company says its exports are up, five questions help:

  1. Are exports up in number of units, or only in value?
  2. What is the operating margin, and is it rising or being squeezed?
  3. How much of the revenue comes from one customer?
  4. How many days of sales are tied up in working capital, and are government incentives still unpaid?
  5. How much is made in India and how much is imported, and is the company building the parts plant or relying on someone else's?
Is your investment plan Market Smart? A Market Smart investor reads a headline growth number the way this article did: what is counted, how much is price, and who actually earns the margin. A 44% jump in exports is a fact about a country. It is not yet a fact about any one share.

What this does not tell you

  • US import data end in August 2026. September and October are the iPhone-launch months, when China's share of US phone imports normally jumps back, so a rebound there would not mean a reversal.
  • A value is not a volume. Unit counts here are for phones entering the US only.
  • The country of origin on a phone is where it was assembled, not where its parts, design and profit come from.
  • It does not say whether any share is cheap or dear. That is a different question.

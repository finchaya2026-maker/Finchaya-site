The RBI Raised the Repo Rate. Higher EMI or Longer Tenure on Your Home Loan?

12 November 2026 · 6 min read

On 7 October 2026 the RBI raised the repo rate by 0.25 percentage point, from 5.25% to 5.50%. News reports call it the first increase since February 2023. If you have a floating-rate home loan, your lender will pass this on at your next rate reset, and you will be asked, or told, how: a higher EMI, or the same EMI for more years.

The short answer. Take ₹50 lakh still owed, 20 years left, and a rate that goes from 8.50% to 8.75%. Raise the EMI and you pay ₹794 more a month and about ₹1.91 lakh more interest over the loan. Keep the EMI and the loan runs about 13 months longer, with about ₹5.40 lakh more interest: nearly three times as much. A higher EMI always costs less in total. A longer tenure only eases this month's budget.

How a rate rise reaches your loan

Most floating-rate home loans today are linked to the repo rate. Your rate is that benchmark plus a margin written into your loan agreement. When the repo rate rises by 0.25, your rate rises by 0.25, and your margin stays where it was.

It does not happen on the day of the announcement. Under the RBI's rules, the rate on a repo-linked loan has to be reset at least once every three months, so the change reaches you at your loan's next reset date. Older loans linked to a bank's own MCLR wait for the bank's next MCLR revision and then the loan's own reset period. Your loan agreement, or your lender's letter, says which date applies to you.

When it does reach you, the RBI's rules on floating-rate resets ask lenders to tell you how the change affects your EMI and your tenure, and to give you a choice: a higher EMI, a longer tenure, or a mix of the two, and the option to move to a fixed rate. Experts quoted in the press say many lenders extend the tenure if you do not reply. So if you stay silent, the choice may be made for you.

Why a longer tenure costs more

Every EMI first pays that month's interest, and what is left repays the loan itself. If the rate goes up and the EMI does not, a bigger share of each EMI goes to interest, the balance falls more slowly, and the loan needs extra months at the end. Those extra months are paid in full EMIs.

Why does skipping ₹794 a month cost so much? The money you do not pay is still borrowed, at 8.75%, and it keeps growing for years. You pay it back, with that growth, as extra months at the end. That is why the bill is ₹5.40 lakh, not ₹1.91 lakh: a gap of ₹3.49 lakh.

How this sits beside the 2025 cuts

In 2025 the RBI cut the repo rate four times, from 6.50% to 5.25%, a total of 1.25 points. If those cuts reached this loan in full (9.75% down to 8.50%), the EMI would have fallen from ₹47,426 to ₹43,391, which is ₹4,035 a month less. The October rise takes back ₹794 of that, about one-fifth. How much of a cut or rise reaches your loan depends on your lender and loan type.

What you can do

  1. Find three numbers. The balance you owe, the years left, and your current rate. Your lender's app or latest statement has them.
  2. Look for your lender's message about the new EMI and tenure, and note your reset date.
  3. Choose on purpose. A higher EMI costs less overall. The same EMI keeps this month's budget steady but costs more later. Some lenders also allow a part-way mix. Which suits you depends on your own cash flow.
  4. If you stretch the tenure, a part-payment can bring the end date back. In this example, about ₹9,900 once a year (₹9,924, paid at each year-end) brings the loan back to 20 years. Floating-rate home loans to individuals generally carry no prepayment charge, but check your lender's terms.

What this does not tell you

  • It assumes the new rate lasts for the rest of the loan. Rates move both ways, so your real total will differ.
  • The rates are examples, not any lender's rate card, and your lender's figures can differ a little by rounding and reset date.
  • It does not say which option to take. That is a decision about your budget, not a rule.
